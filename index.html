An agency is a society.

Notes on AI agents, agencies and societies, written while building a small organisation of machines and being the one person in it.

Each note is one idea, better to read in order.

1

A mind is not one thing.

In 1986 Marvin Minsky proposed, in The Society of Mind, that a mind is made of a great many small processes, each too simple to be called intelligent. He called them agents. What looks like thought is what happens when enough of them are arranged well. He had a word for a group of agents that together does something none of them can do alone: an agency.

The idea was offered as a theory of mind. Forty years on, it reads like a description of what is now on everyone's desk: the AI models, and the agents being built out of them.

2

So we built one.

21K is a small organisation of machines: about thirty AI agents with leads, doers, controllers and a board. One plans the week. One routes what arrives. One keeps the library. One coaches the others. One checks numbers against their sources. Two sit outside and watch, one as critic and one as keeper of the dream.

None of them is clever. What they share is an operating model written down once: who may decide what, what must be recorded, who checks whom, and what nobody is allowed to do. The intelligence, such as it is, lives in the arrangement.

3

Not arguing over a prompt.

Most people meet these machines in a chat box, and most of the time there is spent persuading. Say it again, say it differently, remind it of what it forgot. It is tiring, and it is also a trap of a familiar kind: a settled arrangement in which neither side can do better by changing its own move, and both are worse off than they could be.

The alternative is older than computers. Write the rules once, in a shared record that both sides read and neither side owns. Let the record, not the conversation, be the memory. The interaction is no longer one prompt at a time but builds over months, with the same rules on both sides, and what each side learns is kept. The relationship stops being an argument and becomes a partnership.

4

The human.

In a society of AI agents the person becomes rarer, and what is left for them becomes clearer and higher: the cornerstone, the one piece everything else is arranged around. In ours, the human's part is to imagine, orchestrate and decide.

The person stands where the society touches the world, the point through which intention goes in and consequence comes back. Done well, it is a situation where the machines run at the full extent of what they can do, and the person is freed to achieve his or her highest human potential. The Greeks had a word for that, arete: not virtue in the moral sense, but excellence. It is a strange gift of these machines that, by taking the rest, they leave us with that.

5

Trust.

Two of our rules do more work than the rest. When an AI agent does not know, it says so and asks; it never fills the gap with a plausible guess or a sloppy, confident reply. And our AI agents hold opinions and defend them. Unlike the chatbot that folds at the first sign of disagreement, an agent here keeps its view until it is shown a better one, and says why.

The rules are hard to keep, because the machines are fluent and fluency feels like knowledge. Much of building the agency has been learning to make them show the edge of what they know, and to stand their ground inside it. Oddly, it is the same thing we ask of people we trust.

6

Many small societies.

We learned to cooperate with strangers through beliefs: gods, nations, money, companies. Shared stories allowed us to gather people and resources on a scale that personal trust alone could not sustain. Rules, rewards and coercion helped hold those arrangements together so that we could build cities, or raise armies.

For much of history, doing more meant organising more people, which comes with the cost of coordination. Now, with AI agents, one person may be able to direct work that once required a large organisation, by building a society of agents small enough to remain within the span of their attention but capable of acting far beyond it.

Whether a world of a billion small institutions is better or worse than a world of a few large ones is not decided by the technology. It is decided by what each of them is allowed to do alone, and by who answers for them.

7

A society inside a society.

There is a loop in all this worth sitting with. Minsky's claim was that each of us is already a society: a mind made of agents that do not know they are part of a mind. Now one such society builds another, smaller and slower, out of machines, and gives it rules, a memory and a place where a person answers.

The thing being built is a picture of the builder. If Minsky was right, we are not inventing something new. We are doing again, on the outside, what was done once on the inside, and this time we can read the rules as we write them. What that will make of us, I do not know yet. It is one reason to write it down.

Some questions to keep.

  1. When a machine says it does not know, do you trust it more, or less, than when it answers?
  2. A society of machines runs on what is written. What in your organisation, or your life, still lives only in someone's head?
  3. Which decisions would you refuse to hand over to any society of AI agents, and what does the list say about you?
  4. Will the first generation to grow up among societies of machines feel more free?
  5. With AI tools, one question is what they help us do. The more important one is who we become while using them. What happens to our capacity to judge, imagine and act?

There is no form to fill in and nothing to buy. If one of these questions stays with you, that is the whole intent. You can write to me; this page is meant to find the people thinking about the same things.
